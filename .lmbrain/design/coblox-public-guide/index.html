How Coblox works

Coblox is a network made of ordinary devices. This page explains what it does, what your device does inside it, and what it records about you — without assuming you have read a protocol specification.

Read it straight through and you will have the whole shape of it. The blocks marked + open on the exact version of something the paragraph above has already told you. They are for people who want the mechanism; nothing that matters to a decision is hidden inside one.

01 What is Coblox?

It is a network of ordinary machines — laptops, desktops, spare computers, Android phones — that keep pieces of each other's data and run small programs for each other. No company owns it. There is no central server that everything passes through; if you switch your device off, the network is smaller and nothing else changes.

The devices share one register of who did what. A rotating group of them, called validators, write the register; everybody else can read it and check the writing. The programs that run on the network are called apps, and anybody can publish one.

Plainly

Coblox is good at stopping forgery and not at stopping crowds. It can prove that a balance was not invented, that a signature is genuine, that nothing was spent twice. It cannot tell a thousand devices apart from one computer pretending to be a thousand devices. That is a deliberate and permanent property of this version, not a gap waiting for a patch.

Why the crowd problem is not solved, and what is used instead

Joining costs a one-time proof of work: your device has to do a deliberately expensive memory-heavy calculation before it is enrolled. That makes creating a thousand identities a thousand times as expensive as creating one, and no more than that.

A one-time cost cannot price something that pays out for ever. However hard the joining puzzle is made, an identity that keeps earning eventually pays for itself. So the containment is economic rather than cryptographic: the reward for merely existing is a fixed pot shared out among everyone who qualifies, not an amount per device. Ten fake devices do not create more credits; they only take smaller shares of the same pot — including from you.

Nothing in the protocol attests that a key belongs to a distinct physical machine or to a person. Answering “are you there?” proves that a key is online, not that a device exists.

02 What does your device actually do?

It stays on and connected, and it answers questions it cannot see coming. Another node asks it to prove it is reachable, or to prove it still holds a piece of data it agreed to keep, or to run a small computation and hand back the result. Each question has a deadline. An answer that does not arrive in time counts as an answer that did not arrive.

You do not choose which questions you get. Which device is asked, and the randomness the question is drawn from, are derived rather than picked: the asker has to lock in its secret before the randomness that picks the question exists, so it cannot go looking for a device it already knows will fail. One of the three kinds is not like the other two. For a computation challenge the asker supplies the code and the input itself, so it does choose what you are asked to compute, even though it does not choose whom to ask. That is a known gap in this version, and it is recorded as one rather than left for a reader to discover.

On a phone this is meant to be survivable: the work is sampled rather than continuous, and the network is not allowed to reward a device for simply burning more electricity than its neighbour.

How a question is made unpredictable, and where that stops

The asker publishes a commitment to a secret. Later, a finalised block supplies public randomness. The actual question is derived from both, and the secret is only revealed afterwards, together with the evidence. Every reader — not only validators — recomputes all of it, and evidence that fails any step cannot pay anybody.

The limit, stated with its size: someone who proposes blocks and colludes with the asker can search for a favourable question, because a block's timestamp has some legal room and each value produces different randomness at the cost of one hash. The remaining defence is that every device is questioned by at least two independent askers per period, so the attack is reduced from “pass the question” to “pass one of two”. It is a reduction, not a fix, and it is written down as one.

03 What do you get for it?

Credits. They are written the way a measurement is written — 1 240cr, with the unit after the number, the way you write 512GB — because that is what they are. A credit records that the network used your machine. There is no transaction in Coblox that takes credits from one person and gives them to another: that shape does not exist in the register, so credits cannot be sent, and they are not a currency the network can move around.

You are paid in two ways. Some of it is for work that was checked: storage you proved you still hold, computation you actually ran. Some of it is for being present and reachable at all, and that part is a fixed pot for each period, divided among everyone who qualified.

A period here is a fixed number of blocks, not a stretch of time. That distinction runs through the whole page, so it is worth one paragraph. The register has no clock of its own: every timestamp in it is written by the validators themselves, so how fast the chain really runs is something the network measures afterwards rather than something its rules impose. Blocks are meant to arrive a few seconds apart, and nothing in the rules makes them. So the pot is capped for each period, which is not the same as capped per week: a set of validators that produces blocks faster settles more periods in a day, and mints the pot once for each of them.

Plainly

Your balance is public. It sits in a register that anyone can read, next to your node identifier, permanently. Nobody can take credits away from you — the only entries that reduce your balance are ones you signed yourself — but “nobody can take them” and “nobody can see them” are different sentences, and only the first one is true.

And that identifier is a pseudonym, not a disguise. It carries no name, no email address and no postal address, but it never changes for as long as you keep the same key, so everything it ever does stays tied together under it. The design is pseudonymous, not anonymous. A stable pseudonym is a weak one.

What “a fixed pot, divided” means for the amount you see

For each period the network sets one total for presence income. Everyone who met the threshold that period gets that total divided by their number, rounded down. The remainder is not minted at all, so the period's presence emission is capped by construction rather than by anyone's restraint.

Read that ceiling for what it is. It caps what one period mints, and no rule fixes how many periods fit into a day. The band the network is measured against admits a chain running up to twice as fast as intended before it objects, so the limit on what is minted per period is tighter than the limit on what is minted per week.

Two consequences worth knowing. Your presence income falls when more devices qualify, without anybody doing anything wrong. And because the count is a divisor, it is committed to the actual list of qualifying devices, so an inflated count can be contradicted by recomputation instead of merely disputed.

Why “nobody can take your credits” is exactly, and only, true

Two kinds of entry can lower your balance, and both require your signature: paying for an app subscription, and moving credits into an app's funding account. There is no entry that confiscates, and there is no fee.

One thing this does not cover, and it is the reason the sentence is worded this way: credits you have moved into an app's funding account are no longer yours to hold. From there, the validators spend them on that app's hosting on a schedule, without asking you again. That is what funding an app means.

04 What can you do with them?

You subscribe to apps. An app declares its own subscription price; you pay it, and the credits are destroyed rather than handed over. The people whose machines actually host the app are paid separately by the protocol, once they have proved they did the work.

Plainly

A subscription is a public and permanent fact. Paying for one writes your node identifier, the app, the amount and the dates into the shared register, where they stay for ever and can be read by anyone, and joined up with everything else that identifier ever did. The register is immutable, so there is no way to take it back later. Credits having no monetary value does not change this: what is exposed is not money, it is what you use.

Publishing an app is the other direction. You fund an account attached to the app, and the network bills that account for hosting at a price the validators set — a publisher cannot set or lower its own hosting price. If the account runs dry the app goes into a grace period, then is suspended; it is never silently deleted, and funding it brings it back. If people subscribe to your app, you are paid for that separately, capped so that you cannot profitably subscribe to yourself.

An app you install gets nothing by default. It can only touch what its manifest asks for and you agreed to: a private folder of its own, a monotonic clock, a fixed list of HTTPS addresses. Anything not asked for is denied, and asking is not the same as getting.

The full statement of what the network publishes about you

This page states the fact; it is not the statement. The project has committed to publishing one document that sets out, in full, what is public, permanent and joinable — node identifier, balance, subscriptions and activity times — and it is due before the network admits its first participant from outside the project. It is written once and referred to, rather than restated on every surface, because two copies of a security statement drift apart.

Until it is published, what you are reading here and the project's SECURITY.md are the whole of what has been said, and this paragraph is here so that the gap is visible rather than merely unmentioned.

05 Who keeps the books?

A group of nodes called validators. A block of entries counts as settled when more than two thirds of their voting power has signed it — strictly more, not two thirds exactly.

Two thirds is what it takes to sign a block. It is not what it takes to control the group that signs: a coalition that drives the set into a lawful contraction holds the quorum of the smaller set from about four ninths of the original one, and never has to break a rule to get there. Four ninths is the number that matters for capture; it is smaller than the one most people would guess, and the manoeuvre is set out in full in SECURITY.md.

They are not a permanent committee. Every seat carries an expiry stamped on it when it is filled, so a set with V members and terms of T periods gives up at least V divided by T seats every period. That certainty is counted in blocks, not in weeks: a third of the validators can withhold the quorum a block needs, slow the chain down, and so stretch how long their own terms last in real time without breaking a single rule. Rotation is arithmetic against inertia; against a set that means to stay, it is measured rather than prevented. A departing member sits out a cooling-off period and then competes for re-entry like anyone else. Nobody can be made a validator without standing for it, and the sitting validators cannot name their successors: they can narrow the field of candidates, but not choose who is drawn from it, and how many seats can change hands at one boundary is limited by a cap applied after the draw.

Getting in is a threshold, not a league table. You qualify by having proved storage and computation — not by having been switched on, which is what a rented server in a data centre beats a real phone at. Above the threshold, more storage buys nothing: no seat, no better odds, no extra weight. Every validator counts as exactly one.

Why the threshold is deliberately not a ranking

A rule that pays more for spending more, when the network does not need more, is mining under another name, and the project has excluded mining on principle. A ranking by contributed work would have reintroduced exactly that spending race under the heading of eligibility, so eligibility is a yes-or-no test and voting power is uniform.

The honest size of what this buys: qualifying is expensive to fake, not impossible to fake. The score must draw on several independent askers, which raises the price of faking it in proportion, and does not remove it. Somebody willing to enrol that many colluding identities per fabricated candidate can still clear the threshold. Writing “cannot be faked without spending real resources” would claim more than the rules deliver.

06 What does the network know about you?

The three uncomfortable facts on this page have already been said, and they belong together, so here they are in one place: the network cannot tell a crowd of fake devices from real ones; your balance and your subscriptions are public and permanent; and your identifier is a stable pseudonym, which is a weak one. None of the three is a defect being worked on. They are properties of the design, and the reason they are on this page is that you cannot decide about them after you have joined.

One thing that is not in the register is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to over-promise. Your network address is never written into the shared register, and the key your device uses to make connections is deliberately a different key from the one that identifies you, so that nobody can work out one from the other by reading the register alone. But every node you actually talk to sees the address you are connecting from, and your messages carry your node identifier in the clear. The register does not publish the link. Participating exposes it.

What the register does not let you undo

The register only ever grows. A validator quorum can revoke an identity — for a compromised key, for misconduct, or because its owner asked — and a replacement identity inherits nothing: no balance, no history, no standing. But revocation removes a key from taking part in consensus and from enrolling again; it does not remove what that key did. Nothing you sign can erase evidence or consensus history, including your own signature.

This is the reason the statement about subscriptions has to arrive before you subscribe rather than after. On a register that cannot be edited, telling you afterwards is describing data that has already been published.

07 What happens if somebody cheats?

Mostly, the cheating simply does not count. Evidence of work that fails any of its checks cannot pay anybody, and every reader can run those checks, not only validators. A block that contains a single entry which does not execute is rejected whole: there are no half-applied blocks.

There is no punishment that takes credits away. The register has a closed list of entry types and none of them confiscates. Cheating costs you what you would have been paid, and a validator that misbehaves can have its identity revoked by the others and loses its seat — then has to sit out the cooling-off period like any departing member.

The kind of cheating the design does not stop is the kind described in the first section: not forging anything, just being many. That is why the pot for presence income is capped for each period and shared, and why becoming a validator is anchored to work that is hard to fake rather than to being switched on.

What a device with very little power can check for itself

A phone does not download the whole register. It holds a recent checkpoint signed by a key it already trusts, follows the chain of validator sets forward from it, checks the signatures at each step, and then verifies one compact proof for the one account it cares about.

It fails closed rather than open: a checkpoint that is missing, too old, from the wrong chain, or signed by an unknown key stops the process instead of being ignored. And it refuses to go backwards — it will not accept a lower height than the highest it has already trusted, nor a different block at a height it has already seen.

It can also check something no signature tells it: whether the chain is being run too fast. It counts the blocks that have gone by since its checkpoint and compares them with the time that has gone by on the one clock no validator writes — the moment that checkpoint was issued — against a band fixed when the chain was created. The band is loaded from the device's own copy and may never be learned from a peer, because a peer running the chain fast would hand over a band that says fast is fine.

Too fast, and the phone stops: nothing honest makes blocks appear. Too slow, and it reports rather than refuses, because a reading that looks slow is indistinguishable from a device that has not caught up. This is the one defence on this page you can exercise entirely on your own hardware, without trusting anybody to tell you the truth.

About this page

There are 76 statements of property on this page, and each one is tied to the rule in the protocol specification that holds it, by an automated check that runs on every change. If a rule is changed or removed, the check fails and names this page. The count is written out instead of the word every because each tie is written by hand, one sentence at a time, and a sentence can outgrow the tie that was written for it. That is the whole of the maintenance promise: there is no promise to remember.

Where a rule holds less than a simple sentence would suggest, this page says the smaller thing. The test used throughout: if the simple version is stronger than the exact one, the simple version is wrong.
